03 | Investment method: what WCM is really betting on

3.1 From company research to portfolio construction

The officially disclosed framework includes moats, corporate culture, structural growth, concentrated investment and valuation. In essence it is bottom-up company selection, not a simple replication of a global equity index. [S2]

Research dimensionThis report's professional readingThe counter-question that must be tested
Moat Find companies whose competitive advantage can strengthen and convert into long-term cash flow. Does the advantage come from genuine customer stickiness, or from short-term supply tightness?
Corporate culture Treat management incentives, talent organisation and execution as clues to long-term growth. Can the culture judgment be falsified by operating results, or does it only form a pretty narrative?
Structural growth Find demand that expands over the long term, rather than relying on a single quarter's cycle. Does order growth convert into revenue, profit and free cash flow?
Concentrated investment Let a small number of high-conviction judgments matter enough to the portfolio. Do different companies in fact depend on the same capital-expenditure or financing cycle?
Valuation Even a good company needs a reasonable entry price. Is earnings growth already fully priced into the share price?

3.2 Position management: conviction-driven, not equal-weighted

An official December 2024 investment case study describes that new positions typically start at about 2%-5%, high-conviction holdings can be raised to about 10%, positions are trimmed when they rise into limits, and holdings are adjusted or sold when the competitive advantage weakens. This is a description of operations in a historical case, not proof of the current position of each stock. [S3]

Research inference: This is a strategy that depends on both stock selection and position sizing. When it works, a few winners can lift returns materially; when it fails, a few large errors can magnify losses. Whether the manager adds after a price fall proves nothing by itself; the key is whether the investment thesis is still supported by operating data.

3.3 A cautious appraisal of the method

  • Strength: the framework is distinctive and can form a portfolio that differs from the index.
  • Weakness: culture and moat judgments are subjective and need clear falsification indicators.
  • Not yet proven: whether the current drawdown is a temporary style headwind or the failure of some stock-selection theses.

In principle the fund does not hedge currency exposure back into yen. If foreign currencies depreciate against the yen, the yen value of foreign-currency assets is reduced. [S1]

This report does not use USD/JPY as a proxy for the entire currency exposure: a formal forecast should model the actual denomination currencies of the assets — US dollar, euro, pound, Korean won and others — separately, and confirm the security form used and the valuation exchange rates.

06 | Valuation and fees: a good method does not mean any price is worth paying

6.1 It cannot simply be labelled an "expensive technology fund"

Official end-August 2026 portfolio characteristics disclose a forward P/E of about 14.3x for the mother fund versus about 16.1x for the reference index; trailing P/E was about 22.1x and 20.9x respectively. These are the manager's statistics for that point in time and cannot serve as the portfolio's valuation on November 25. [S4]

Research inference: The historical forward P/E not being high is worth further checking, but it is not proof of cheapness: future earnings forecasts can be revised down, industry structures differ, and earnings cyclicality differs. The calculation method, treatment of loss-makers, forecast horizon and cash share must be verified. This report therefore accepts neither "growth stocks are necessarily expensive" nor "a low forward P/E necessarily means undervalued".

6.2 Fees are a clear investment hurdle

The official prospectus lists an annual trust fee of 1.958% (tax included); the purchase fee is set by the distributor, capped at 3.3%, and there are other costs. The published NAV already has the fund's related fees deducted. [S1]

As a pure comparison assumption, if another product's annual fee were 0.20%, then on the trust-fee difference alone WCM would need roughly 1.758 percentage points of extra return per year to offset that fee gap; this is not a complete after-tax comparison and does not name a specific alternative product.

Preventing double deduction: When using the fund's realised NAV returns, the trust fee must not be deducted a second time; fees need to be added explicitly only when forecasting bottom-up from equities and currencies. The scenario model below is of the latter kind.

7.2 Six-month price model

To avoid prices from thin air, a reproducible simplified model is used:

V* = V0 x {1 + a x [(1 + q) x (1 + f) - 1]} x (1 - c)
  • V0: JPY 9,983, using the user's November 24 screenshot input.
  • a: assumed equity ratio of 90%; not a verified current weight.
  • q: assumed six-month local-currency total return of the equity basket, including dividends.
  • f: assumed weighted foreign-currency move against the yen for the basket; a positive value helps the yen NAV.
  • c: six-month trust-fee approximation of 0.979%, i.e. half of 1.958%.
  • V*: the equivalent NAV if no future fund distributions are paid; not a promised published NAV.

The model simplifies the interaction between currencies and holdings, the fee-deduction path and cash returns; it excludes trading costs, other fees and turnover. q, f and a are all scenario inputs, not historically calibrated.

Price after a distribution, and the investor's total wealth

Vt = V(t-1) x (1 + Rt) - Dt

Rt must be the pre-distribution return including the corresponding fee effects; Dt is the pre-tax distribution per 10,000 units. If the pre-distribution NAV is 9,920 and 100 is distributed, the published NAV becomes about 9,820. That 100 comes from the fund's net assets; it is not newly created wealth. [S1]

Multi-period forecasts must deduct distributions period by period and handle the subsequent return path; subtracting cumulative distributions from the final NAV is not an exact substitute. An individual's actual return also depends on taxes, whether the cash is reinvested, and trading timing; this report does not estimate personal after-tax returns.

09 | Investment-committee decision conditions and risk budgeting

To upgrade to a more positive view, the following must appear together

  1. Obtain the latest official holdings and confirm changes in core positions and risk concentration.
  2. Core investment theses are supported by operating and cash-flow data.
  3. After-fee total return improves, rather than only a brief post-distribution NAV bounce.
  4. Relative performance versus the reference market stops deteriorating.
  5. The entry valuation can absorb errors in earnings forecasts.

Signs that call for a downgrade or re-review

  • Competitive advantage, cash flow or management guidance at core holdings keeps deteriorating.
  • Several holdings are hit simultaneously by a reversal in a common demand cycle.
  • Turnover increases without an explainable logic for the risk and return improvement.
  • Equity declines coincide with yen appreciation.
  • The investor actually needs stable cash flow yet depends on unguaranteed monthly distributions.

Size positions backwards from tolerable loss, not from target return

Illustrative maximum position = tolerable portfolio loss budget / assumed fund stress loss

Pure illustration: if the total-asset loss budget allocated to this investment is 3% and the assumed stress loss is 40%, the arithmetic position is 7.5%. This is not personal allocation advice; correlated holdings falling together, liquidity, and more severe tail losses may require a lower position.

Distribution type vs asset-growth type should be chosen by purpose

Officially, the asset-growth type in principle restrains distributions in pursuit of compounding; the distribution type settles monthly and decides distributions by rule. Their price levels cannot be compared directly as "expensive or cheap". [S1]

The research approach this report recommends is to compare total return, fees and actual cash-flow needs on the same basis, rather than choosing because one NAV is lower or the other's historical distributions were higher.
